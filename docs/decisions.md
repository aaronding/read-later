# Decisions not covered by the brief

The brief says: when a decision isn't covered, choose the simpler option and note it here. Each entry says what was chosen and why. Newest entries are at the bottom.

## Tooling

1. **pnpm 10.28 is pinned** in `package.json` (`packageManager`). It is the version the project was built and tested with. A newer pnpm on the owner's PC will still work; Corepack or pnpm itself switches to the pinned version.
2. **TypeScript 5.9, not 7.** TypeScript 7 (the native compiler) was the newest release, but Next.js 16 type-checks through the JavaScript compiler API, which TypeScript 7 does not provide. 5.9 is the newest release that works with it.
3. **Node 24 is required** (`engines` in the root `package.json`, and `node:24-slim` in the Dockerfile), matching the owner's PC. Some dependencies (the Anthropic SDK, and jsdom 30 in the extension's tests) no longer support early Node 22 releases.
4. **The display name lives in `packages/brand/index.js`** (`APP_NAME`). The web app imports it. The Chrome extension's build script copies it into the extension manifest, and the extension reads it back at runtime with `chrome.runtime.getManifest()`. Changing that one constant renames everything.

## Data and storage

5. **Plain `pg` (node-postgres) everywhere**, including against Neon, rather than Neon's serverless driver. Neon accepts ordinary TCP connections, so the same code works on Vercel, on a server and against a local Postgres.
6. **Better Auth tables are plural** (`users`, `sessions`, ...) via the adapter's `usePlural` option, so raw SQL never has to quote the reserved word `user`.
7. **HTML sent by the extension or Shortcut is kept in `articles.submitted_html` only until it is extracted**, then cleared. Keeping raw pages would fill Neon's 1 GB quickly.
8. **One summary row per article and model** (unique on both). The summary shown in lists and the reader comes from the model in `articles.summary_model`: the default model at save time, or the last model chosen with "Re-summarize with…".

## Saving and duplicates

9. **Only the tracking parameters the brief names are removed** (`utm_*`, `fbclid`, `gclid`, case-insensitive). The other parameters are kept byte-for-byte and in order, because some sites use them to pick the page.
10. **"Same site" for `rel=canonical` means the same host, ignoring a leading `www.`**, without the public suffix list. It is simpler, and it errs on the side of not trusting a canonical.
11. **When duplicates are detected.** At save time we use the URL and, if the browser sent HTML, its trusted canonical. After a server-side download we also use the canonical, or the final URL after redirects (so a shortened link matches the real page). The stored key is changed only if no other saved article already uses it; the two are not merged. Every one of these addresses is remembered, and a save matches any of them (113).
12. **A re-save with full HTML replaces the stored copy when that copy is "Preview only", failed or still empty,** and a re-save by its address downloads such a copy again (114). A re-save never replaces a complete copy, and a preview saved again gets new summaries only when the new copy is better, so nothing is summarized twice by accident.
13. **`POST /api/save` allows any origin (CORS `*`).** It authenticates with a bearer token, not cookies, so other sites can't use the owner's session.
14. **Access tokens are `rl_` followed by 256 random bits, stored as a SHA-256 hash.** That is enough for random tokens; password-style hashing is for low-entropy secrets.
15. **Removing an email from `ALLOWED_EMAILS` locks that person out everywhere**: pages, new sign-ins, and their access tokens.
16. **Submitted HTML is limited to 4 MB** (`SAVE_MAX_HTML_BYTES`), under Vercel's 4.5 MB request body limit.

## Downloads and the paywall rules

17. **Download limits:** 15 seconds in total, 10 MB after decompression (a CNN article is 5.6 MB, mostly scripts and styles), at most 5 redirects, HTML content types only (PDFs and images are not saved in stage 1). 404 and 410 fail at once; 408, 429 and 5xx are retried; 401, 402 and 403 are kept and flagged "Preview only", with the status in the reason: a 403 can be a site turning away downloads that don't come from a browser, not only a login.
18. **Redirects and DNS are checked on every hop** by doing the address check inside the socket's DNS lookup, so a hostname can't pass the check and then connect to a private address.
19. **Paywall markup is applied only to our own downloads.** Publishers keep `isAccessibleForFree: false` in pages they serve to subscribers too, so a copy from the owner's signed-in browser is kept in full. The truncation and login heuristics apply to both.
20. **Previews of a login page, an empty page or a very short text are not summarized.** Previews that hold the free beginning of an article (subscriber-only or a paywall teaser) are summarized and the summary is marked partial.

## Summaries (Claude)

21. **The initial default model is `claude-opus-5-5`**, the claude-api skill's default. It can be changed in Settings, or for new users with `DEFAULT_SUMMARY_MODEL`.
22. **Effort `low` by default** (`SUMMARY_EFFORT`). The skill recommends `low` for summarization and extraction. Thinking is adaptive (the only mode these three models allow), and `max_tokens` is 16,000 so thinking has room.
23. **Requests stream** (`client.beta.messages.stream(...).finalMessage()`), as the skill recommends for long inputs.
24. **Refusal fallbacks are on for Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5** (`fallbacks: "default"`, beta `server-side-fallback-2026-07-01`). Haiku 5.5 has no server-side fallback, so the parameter is left off there. A refusal is recorded and not retried.
25. **Structured output uses a plain JSON schema, and the result is validated in our code.** The SDK's auto-parsing throws before `stop_reason` can be checked, so a refusal or a cut-off reply would surface as a parse error. Structured outputs can't enforce "3 to 5" or "1 to 3" items, so the counts are enforced after parsing.
26. **The model also returns `language`** (a BCP 47 tag), which the UI uses to mark the summary's language.
27. **Existing tags are reused exactly as written, even in another language.** Preferring existing tags is meant to avoid near-duplicates, and that would fail if every Chinese article got a Chinese twin of an English tag. New tags are written in the article's language.
28. **Input limit: 200,000 characters** (`SUMMARY_MAX_INPUT_CHARS`), about 50K tokens of English. Longer articles are cut at a paragraph break, Claude is told it has only the beginning, and the summary is marked partial with the character counts.
29. **Cost is an estimate** from the token counts and a price table in `apps/web/src/config/models.ts`. When a refusal falls back to another model, every attempt is counted at its own model's price, which may overstate the cost.
30. **No prompt caching.** Each request is a different article, and the fixed system prompt is below the minimum cacheable length.
31. **Retries:** each job gets 5 attempts with backoff of 30 s, 2 min, 8 min and 32 min (±20%). The SDK retries once inside an attempt with a 90-second timeout, so one attempt fits in a 300-second serverless function. A running job whose worker disappears is taken back after 10 minutes.

## Dependencies

32. **Every dependency must have been published for at least 7 days** (`minimumReleaseAge` in `pnpm-workspace.yaml`, which also covers indirect dependencies). Compromised npm releases are usually caught within days. When the project was set up, this meant using the newest versions at least a week old, for example Anthropic SDK 0.133 instead of 1.0.0 (released that day) and Better Auth 1.7.7 instead of 1.7.12. The cost is getting fixes a week later; a security fix that can't wait can be pinned on purpose and noted here.

## Web app

33. **Plain CSS** in one stylesheet (`apps/web/src/app/globals.css`), rather than a CSS framework. Its colors and reading font are CSS variables that follow the reader's appearance choices (79 to 82).
34. **Articles are marked read when the reader page has loaded in the browser**, not during server rendering, so Next.js link prefetching can't mark an article read that was never opened.
35. **Triage shows unread Inbox articles, newest first.** "Later" keeps the article in the Inbox and moves it to the back of the triage queue (`articles.triaged_at`). "Read now" opens the reader.
36. **Polling:** pages with work in progress refresh themselves every 3 seconds while visible, and stop after 5 minutes so a job that is waiting out its retry delay doesn't poll forever.
37. **Search** uses Postgres full-text search with the language-neutral `simple` configuration, because saved articles come in many languages. Each word matches as a prefix ("orchard" finds "orchards"); there is no stemming. Postgres doesn't split Chinese or Japanese text into words, so queries in those scripts also match as substrings of the title, text and summary.
38. **The header search searches the list you are looking at** (Inbox, Favorites or Archive); from other pages it searches the Inbox.
39. **The paste box accepts bare addresses** such as `example.com/story` and reads them as `https://`.
40. **Tags are case-insensitive per user and reused as written.** Removing a tag from its last article deletes the tag, so it stops steering Claude's suggestions. Folders are flat, and deleting one keeps its articles.
41. **Export** produces one JSON file (all fields, text, sanitized HTML, every summary with its tokens and cost) or one Markdown file (metadata, the shown summary and the plain text of each article).
42. **The reader drops a heading at the top of the article that repeats the title**, since the title is already shown above. Readability only does this when the heading matches its own guess at the title, which may still include the site name.
43. **Security headers:** a nonce-based Content Security Policy, set per request in `apps/web/src/proxy.ts`. Only scripts carrying that request's nonce run (Next.js adds it to its own scripts), so even a script that got past the sanitizer into a saved article would be blocked. Frames and plugins are blocked; images from any host are allowed, because articles use them. Styles stay `'unsafe-inline'`, because style attributes can't carry a nonce, and saved articles have their styles removed anyway. Also `nosniff`, `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer` and `X-Frame-Options: DENY`.

## Saving from devices

44. **Android's share target uses GET** (`/share?url&text&title`), handled by a route handler that saves and then redirects to the Inbox. Sign-in cookies are reliably sent on a top-level GET; with POST they may not be, depending on the browser's SameSite handling of share-target navigations. A link to `/share` on another website could otherwise make the owner save pages (and spend Claude credits) without knowing. So when the browser reports that another site sent it (`Sec-Fetch-Site: cross-site` or `same-site`), the app asks for confirmation first. Shares from the Share menu are browser-initiated and save directly.
45. **Two Apple Shortcuts instead of one.** One for Safari, which runs JavaScript to send the whole page; one for other apps, which sends the link. Shortcuts has no reliable way for one shortcut to tell a Safari page from a plain URL.
46. **The extension relies on CORS rather than host permissions** to call the server, so it asks for no access to any website. `GET /api/save` with a token answers "Connected" so the options page can check the setup.
47. **If a page's HTML is too large to send** (over the 4 MB limit), the extension saves the link alone and says so.

## Infrastructure

48. **One Docker image for the web app, the worker and the migrations**, chosen by the command (`web`, `worker`, `migrate`). It includes development dependencies (the worker runs TypeScript through `tsx`). The image is larger, about 1.3 GB, but it is simpler than a separate build for each role. The app runs as the unprivileged `node` user.
49. **Compose starts `cloudflared` only with the `tunnel` profile** (`COMPOSE_PROFILES=tunnel` in the server's `.env`), so the same file also works on a PC without a tunnel token. The web container listens on the server's `127.0.0.1:3000` only.
50. **Postgres 17** in Compose and for backups, matching the Neon project. Neon's default for new projects became 18 in June 2026, so the Neon guide says to pick 17. 17 is the version PGlite (used by the tests) is built on, and moving data between Neon and the server stays a plain dump and restore.
51. **Backups:** compressed `pg_dump` custom-format files in R2 under `daily/`. Deleting old copies is left to an R2 lifecycle rule (30 days is suggested). The GitHub Action skips with a notice until its secrets exist. On the one-server setup, `scripts/server-backup.sh` runs from the server's crontab instead, because GitHub can't reach a database with no open ports.
52. **The cron Worker has no public URL** (`workers_dev = false`); it only runs on its schedule, every 5 minutes.
53. **One `.env` at the repository root** configures the web app, the worker, the migrations and Compose. Variables already set in the environment take precedence.
54. **`scripts/mock-claude.ts`** is a local stand-in for the Claude API, so the whole flow can be tried without an API key and without cost. It isn't used in production.
55. **Line endings:** `.gitattributes` keeps LF endings for shell scripts and Docker files, so a Windows checkout still builds a working image.
56. **`apps/web/AGENTS.md` is committed.** `next dev` writes it to point coding agents at the Next.js docs bundled in `node_modules`, and it re-creates the file whenever it's missing.


## After the security review

57. **A re-save while a job is running gets its own run.** Only *queued* jobs count as "already pending"; a running job has already read the old state. Submitted HTML is cleared after processing only if it is still the HTML that was processed (compared by MD5 in SQL), so a newer copy that arrives meanwhile is kept for its own run.
58. **An article and its summary requests are stored in one transaction**, and a summary is never marked pending without a job. Retry also picks up summaries stuck as pending or running with no job, and the reader offers Retry when a summary has said "Summarizing…" for over 10 minutes.
59. **A job's result is recorded only while its worker still holds the job.** If a worker took so long that another worker took the job back, the slow worker's late result is dropped.
60. **Each Claude call has an overall limit of 150 seconds** (`SUMMARY_TIMEOUT_MS`). The SDK's own timeout stops counting once a streamed answer starts, so a stalled stream could otherwise outlive a 300-second serverless function. A call that hits the limit is retried later.
61. **A summary that ran out of tokens (`max_tokens`) is not retried automatically**, because the same request would most likely stop the same way; Retry in the app tries again on purpose.
62. **After a refusal fallback, only the text after the last `fallback` marker is read.** The declined model's partial answer comes before it.
63. **Redirect targets** (`?next=` on sign-in, and the reader's delete) are resolved against a placeholder origin and must stay on it. Values with backslashes or control characters are refused, because browsers read `/\evil.com` as another site.
64. **Saved articles' `id` and `name` attributes get a `user-content-` prefix** (DOMPurify's `SANITIZE_NAMED_PROPS`), and links to anchors inside the article are rewritten to match. Without the prefix, an element such as `<a id="__next_f">` could shadow a page global and break the reader.

## Neon

65. **`neon.ts` at the repository root declares Postgres only**, as `neon init` wrote it, with `auth: false`: the app keeps its own Better Auth with Google, and Neon's guidance is not to replace a working sign-in. Its branch policy gives branches made with `neon checkout --create` a 7-day lifetime. `@neon/config` is a root dev dependency, since only the Neon CLI reads `neon.ts`. The app doesn't use `@neon/env` (Neon's helper for reading those variables), but `neon init` installs it with `@neon/config` and tries to add it again when it is missing, which fails at a pnpm workspace root, so it stays. `.neon`, which names the linked organization, project and branch, is git-ignored, as `neon init` set it up.
66. **The Neon CLI is installed globally (`npm i -g neon`), not as a dependency**, so pnpm's 7-day rule doesn't reach it. The setup used 8.10.3, published the same day, as Neon's instructions asked for the latest release; the newest week-old one, 8.4.4, wasn't tried with these commands. It is a tool the owner runs by hand, never part of the app, but it holds the Neon login and writes `.env`, so it is worth installing with care. The packages the repository depends on do follow the rule: `@neon/config` 1.9.1, `@neon/env` 1.6.2 and `@vercel/functions` 3.10.1 were the newest week-old releases.
67. **The driver stays node-postgres, with `attachDatabasePool` from `@vercel/functions`**, which Neon and Vercel both recommend for Vercel's Fluid compute: it keeps a function alive just long enough to close its idle connections before Vercel suspends it, and does nothing off Vercel. On Vercel, idle connections close after 5 seconds, Vercel's recommendation; elsewhere after 30. Neon's serverless driver (HTTP or WebSocket) is meant for platforms that can't keep a pool, and the worker and the Docker setup use the same pool.
68. **The app uses Neon's pooled string (`DATABASE_URL`), the migrations and drizzle-kit the direct one (`DATABASE_URL_UNPOOLED`)**, under the names `neon env pull` writes. The pooler runs PgBouncer in transaction mode, which migrations shouldn't go through; the app's queries keep no session state (no `SET`, `LISTEN` or advisory locks), so they work through it. Without `DATABASE_URL_UNPOOLED`, as with a local Postgres, everything uses `DATABASE_URL`. Docker Compose sets both to its own Postgres, so a Neon value left in a server's `.env` can't send the migrations to Neon.
69. **`sslmode=require` reaches node-postgres as `verify-full`.** node-postgres treats `require` as `verify-full` today but warns that its next major version will check less, and Neon recommends `verify-full`. **`channel_binding=require` turns on node-postgres's channel binding** (SCRAM-SHA-256-PLUS when the server offers it), which it otherwise ignores.
70. **Vercel runs the functions in Cleveland (`cle1`, `apps/web/vercel.json`)**, next to the Neon project in AWS US East 2 (Ohio), rather than in Vercel's default of Washington, D.C.

## From the first local run

71. **Downloads name HTTP/1.1 in the TLS handshake (ALPN), as browsers and curl do.** Node offers no protocol there unless asked. CNN answered Node's HTTP/1.1 requests with a 403 whatever their User-Agent or header names, while serving the same request from curl, and from Node over HTTP/2. Offering `http/1.1`, the protocol the downloader speaks, is a standard part of the handshake, not a disguise: the User-Agent still names the app.
72. **Script and style-sheet contents are left out before jsdom builds the DOM**, except JSON-LD, which the paywall rules read. They are most of a big page's bytes and never part of the article: on a CNN article, extraction went from 3.8 to about 1.2 seconds. parse5, the parser jsdom itself uses, finds where each one starts and ends, so a `<script` inside a comment, a title or an attribute is left alone, and hostile markup can't slow it down. The elements and their attributes stay. A page with more than 4 MB of HTML left, the limit for a page sent from the browser, is refused as too large: building a DOM from that much markup already takes about 30 seconds and 1.5 GB.
73. **On a server download, the free part of a subscriber-only article is what is left of the article itself**: the `article` element around the largest removed section, or `main`, or else the nearest wrapper that still holds text, but never the whole `<body>`. Readability runs on the same page narrowed to that part, so the head's title, author and date still count. It no longer searches the rest of the page, where on CNN it took a sidebar of stock prices for the article. When fewer than 100 words are left, no text is kept from the page and nothing is summarized: the reader shows the title and description with the "Preview only" notice, unless the description can take their place (115).
74. **JSON-LD is recognized however its type is written** (`application/ld+json` in any case, with or without parameters such as `; charset=utf-8`), so a page can't escape the paywall rules by the way it labels its markup.

## Firefox

75. **The extension also builds for Firefox**, which the brief doesn't ask for; the owner did. One build writes both, and only `manifest.json` differs, made by `apps/extension/manifest.mjs`. Firefox runs a Manifest V3 background script as an event page (`background.scripts`) rather than a service worker. The code keeps calling `chrome.*`, which Firefox also provides with promises, so it needs no polyfill. Firefox for Android has no right-click menus, so the code uses `chrome.contextMenus` only where it exists.
76. **The add-on ID is fixed, `read-later@aaronding`**, rather than made from `APP_NAME`, so renaming the app can't turn it into a different add-on and lose its settings. Mozilla needs an ID to sign a Manifest V3 add-on.
77. **It declares the data it sends, browsing activity and website content, as required.** Mozilla asks every new add-on to declare this, and saving does send a page's address and content, if only to the owner's own server. Firefox shows the declaration from version 140, and from 142 on Android, so those are the minimum versions.
78. **The Firefox version is signed as an unlisted add-on ("On your own")**: everyday Firefox installs only add-ons Mozilla has signed, and unlisted signing is automatic and publishes nothing, as the Chrome version is loaded unpacked until its store listing in stage 3. Updates are installed by hand; an update server isn't worth it for one person. The build writes the zip itself (`apps/extension/zip.mjs`, with no dependency), since zipping the folder in Windows Explorer puts the folder inside the archive, which Mozilla rejects. Mozilla asks for the source of compiled code, and the guide's source archive holds only the extension, `packages/brand` and the workspace files; it rebuilds the same zip byte for byte.

## Reading design

79. **Themes: System, Light or Dark, with five palettes for each.** Light has Paper (the default: the app's first colors, with an ink accent since 110), White, Sepia, Sage and Mist; dark has Graphite (the default), Black, Slate, Midnight and Dusk. The light and the dark palette are chosen separately, so System switches between two palettes the reader likes. Each palette sets four colors (page, cards, text and accent) in `apps/web/src/lib/appearance.ts`, and the other shades are mixed from them. A test holds every palette to WCAG contrast: 7:1 (AAA) for article and summary text, which people read for minutes at a time, 4.5:1 for all other text, links and buttons, and 3:1 for the focus ring. The root layout puts the resulting CSS variables in the page, so a new palette is one line.
80. **A font for articles and summaries; the rest of the app keeps the device's font.** Classic, the serif the app used before (Iowan Old Style on Apple devices, Palatino on Windows, the default serif on Android), stays the default, and Sans is the device's own UI font. Literata, Source Serif, Merriweather and Atkinson Hyperlegible (made for readers with low vision) come from Google Fonts through `next/font`, which downloads them when the app is built and serves them from the app itself: browsers never contact Google, and the Content Security Policy needs no change. They aren't preloaded, so a browser downloads a font only once it is chosen. A production build has to reach Google Fonts, which Vercel's build and the Docker build can; `next dev` uses a local font when it can't. These fonts cover Latin letters only, so Chinese, Japanese and Korean text uses the device's serif or sans fonts.
81. **The appearance is kept in the account, in its own table (`user_appearance`),** so every device signed in to it reads the same way; the owner asked for this over a choice per browser. It isn't in `user_settings`, because creating that row would also fix the default summary model, which otherwise follows `DEFAULT_SUMMARY_MODEL` until one is chosen. Signed out, as on the sign-in page, pages use the default. The root layout looks the choice up for every page: it colors the browser's bars (`theme-color`), and a one-line inline script in `<head>`, which carries the CSP nonce, puts it on `<html>` as `data-*` attributes before the first paint. React doesn't render those attributes itself: if it did, a refresh already on its way when the reader changed a color (the Inbox refreshes while articles are summarized) would put the old color back. A change shows at once, and a Server Action saves it; the pickers send their saves one after another, so clicking quickly through the choices leaves the last one saved.
82. **The "Aa" button in the header** opens the same choices as Settings over the page, so they can be tried on the article being read; on a phone it is a sheet at the bottom of the screen. It uses the browser's popover, which needs no script to open or close; browsers without popovers (Safari before 17) don't show the button, and Settings still works there. The reader's column holds about 70 characters a line, article text is 18 px on phones and 19 px on larger screens, and while reading, the header scrolls away with the page instead of staying at the top.

## Logo

83. **The logo is 冊, the character for a book, as bronze inscriptions draw it: bamboo slips bound with cord.** Before paper, Chinese books were written on bamboo slips tied together, and the character draws them: four slips of different lengths, and a cord that runs across them and closes in a half circle past the last slip, as Western Zhou bronzes write it. Each saved article is a slip, and the app binds them into a book; the longest slip is a bookmark, with a notched end. The strokes are even, with round ends. The character is a picture as much as a word, so it can be read as bound slips without knowing Chinese, and it doesn't depend on the name. At a glance it can pass for a #, which the cord's loop and the bookmark tell apart. Its colors are in 110. It replaced a bookmark ribbon folded into an L, whose L looked like a snake, and then the same 冊 drawn as if cut into oracle bone, whose strokes narrowed to points the owner didn't want. Also tried and dropped: a bookmark carrying lines of text, as many logos already do; a pilcrow whose two stems end as ribbons; 書 in seal script as a book collector's seal, which shrinks to a framed square at 16 px; 冊 in seal script, five slips and two bowed cords, which crowd together at 16 px; the modern 书 and 读, plain beside the old forms; and a crescent moon on a bookmark, since a pale crescent on green is the emblem on several national flags and a religious symbol.
84. **The logo's sources are SVGs in `packages/brand/logo`, and `pnpm icons` draws every icon from them.** `mark.svg` and `mark-dark.svg` are the logo the pages show (87). `icon.svg` is the app's icon at every size but 16 and 32 px, where `icon-small.svg` takes over: the slips and the cord as 2 px lines on whole pixels of a 32 px grid, without the notch, so the 16 and 32 px icons and the favicon have sharp edges instead of blurred ones. `maskable.svg` fills the square for Android, which crops icons to its own shape, with the character inside the safe zone; `apple-icon.svg` has square corners, which iOS rounds itself. The script is in `apps/web` because it renders with sharp, which Next.js already installs at the same version; as a dependency of `packages/brand`, sharp would also be installed from the Firefox source archive. The PNGs are committed, so a build never runs it.
85. **The Apple touch icon uses Next.js's file convention** (`apps/web/src/app/apple-icon.png`), so adding the app to an iPhone's or iPad's Home Screen shows the logo. The old `public/icons/apple-touch-icon.png` was never linked from any page. Declaring it in `metadata.icons` instead would make Next.js drop the file-based `icon.svg`. The unlinked `favicon-32.png` is gone too: Safari has shown SVG favicons since version 26, like the other browsers.
86. **Next to the logo, the name is set in the app's classic serif** (`--font-brand`: Iowan Old Style, Palatino, Georgia), in the header and on the sign-in page. It is part of the logo, so it doesn't follow the font the reader picks for articles (80). It stays live text from `APP_NAME`, so a new name needs no new artwork.
87. **The logo has a dark version for dark mode** (`mark-dark.svg`): a lighter ink tile (`#34302b`), which still shows on a dark page, with the strokes in bone (`#e3d8c1`) and the bookmark in a lighter cinnabar (`#e5705b`). Its colors stay the same in every palette. The header and the sign-in page show the dark version wherever the dark colors apply: with the theme set to Dark, or to System on a device in dark mode, the same rule as `appearanceCss()` (79). Both SVGs are in the page, under 1 KB each, and CSS shows one of them; the README on GitHub picks with `<picture>`. The favicon follows the browser instead, with a `prefers-color-scheme` rule inside `icon-small.svg` that darkens its cinnabar to `#3f1813` and its strokes to `#f0907d`; sharp ignores that rule, so the PNGs are drawn in the light colors. Home screen and toolbar icons keep the light version, which also reads well on a dark background.

## Languages

88. **The interface comes in English, French, Simplified Chinese and Traditional Chinese, through next-intl** (4.14.9). It is the one internationalization library made for the App Router that takes the language from somewhere other than the URL; Paraglide, Lingui and react-intl need the language in the URL, a compile step or hand-written Next.js glue. next-intl brings `@swc/core` and `@parcel/watcher` for a message extractor the app doesn't use, so pnpm's "Ignored build scripts" warning about them is expected; pnpm 10.28's `ignoredBuiltDependencies` setting didn't silence it.
89. **The language isn't in the URL.** It is the signed-in user's choice in Settings (`user_settings.locale`), else the browser's languages (`Accept-Language`), else English. Signed out, as on the sign-in page, a cookie remembers the last choice made on that browser. Links stay the same in every language, and a personal app has no need for a page per language for search engines. `zh-TW`, `zh-HK` and `zh-MO` mean Traditional Chinese and other `zh` tags Simplified, through `Intl.Locale`'s likely subtags.
90. **Traditional Chinese follows Taiwan's usage** (設定, 登入, 帳號, 資料夾, 儲存) and is its own translation, not a conversion of the Simplified text. Its pages are `lang="zh-Hant-TW"`, so browsers draw Taiwan's character forms and screen readers use a Taiwan voice. Traditional Chinese text, in the interface or in an article, gets Traditional Chinese fonts (PingFang TC, Microsoft JhengHei, Songti TC, Noto CJK TC), in the reading fonts too (80). Browsers set to Hong Kong or Macau Chinese get it as well.
91. **The text is one JSON file per part of the app and per language** (`apps/web/messages/<language>/<Namespace>.json`), in ICU message format, so changes to different screens don't collide. English is the source. A missing translation would show the English text, but a test requires every language to have every message, with the same values, plural and select cases and tags. `pnpm typecheck` runs `next typegen` first, which writes the declarations that check message keys and values against the English files; they aren't committed. Only the parts client components use are sent to the browser.
92. **Errors and "Preview only" reasons saved by background jobs are stored as a message key and its values** (`error_message`, `preview_reason_message`) next to the English text, and translated when shown: a job runs long before anyone reads its result, without knowing their language. Older rows show their English text. Details from elsewhere, such as an HTTP status, the Claude API's own message or Node's network error, are shown as they came.
93. **The save API answers in the token owner's chosen language, else the request's `Accept-Language`**, so the Shortcut's notification matches the app. Its error replies also carry a code for programs: `unauthorized`, `bad_request`, `invalid_url`, `too_large` or `server_error`.
94. **The extension follows the browser's language** (`chrome.i18n`, with `_locales/en`, `fr`, `zh_CN` and `zh_TW`), because an extension can't choose its messages at run time. It shows its own words for the save API's outcomes and codes, so its text stays in one language, and the server's text only for anything it doesn't know. Its messages write the name as `{app}`, which the build fills in from `packages/brand`.
95. **Choosing a language doesn't fix the default summary model.** `user_settings.default_model` may now be empty, and stays so until a model is chosen in Settings, so it keeps following `DEFAULT_SUMMARY_MODEL` (the concern in 81).
96. **Dates follow the app's language in the browser's time zone**, since LocalDate formats them in the browser; anything formatted on the server uses UTC. Numbers and costs (in US dollars) are formatted for the app's language, and the export follows the language of the request.
97. **The French and Chinese texts were written with Claude**, from a shared glossary of the app's terms based on Microsoft's, Apple's and Google's terminology for each language, part by part and then reviewed language by language. The setup guides use other products' own translated labels (Chrome, the Shortcuts app, Android). A native speaker's read is worth having before the public launch.

## Login pages

98. **A password field makes a page a login page only when it belongs to the article,** not wherever it is on the page. Slashdot, LWN, forums (vBulletin, Discuz!, Invision, XOOPS, Tiki) and many news and blog sites carry a sign-in form on every page, in the header, a sidebar or a hidden modal, so their short stories and posts were flagged "Preview only" and never summarized. A field belongs to the article when the elements around it, marked before Readability runs, are still in what Readability takes for the article. It also does when it is visible and beside the article's headline, in a part of the page under 300 words that isn't its header or navigation: that catches registration walls whose form Readability removes once the teaser runs past a few lines, or where Readability takes the footer for the article. The headline is an `h1` that is most of the page's title and isn't the site's name, whether the page's metadata names the site or the `h1` only links to the site's root, as a blog's name often does. When the headline is in an `article` or `main` element, the field must be in it too, so a sign-in box in the sidebar beside a short post, as in Joomla's layout, doesn't count. A field in a comment form never counts: a form with a visible text area, or one named for comments, since FC2 blogs and N-DSoft news sites let a commenter set a password to edit or delete the comment later. The hidden text area reCAPTCHA and hCaptcha put inside sign-in forms doesn't make one a comment form. A form that holds the headline or the main part, or most of the page's text, wraps the whole page, as on ASP.NET sites, so its fields still count. Of 371 pages collected from about 190 sites, 235 have a password field. On those, 43 complete pages from 25 sites are no longer flagged, and every wall the old rule caught still is, except three teasers whose only sign of the wall was the site's usual hidden sign-in box (Law360, SpaceNews, a Discuz! thread that hides replies) and two MyBB "not logged in" pages. Of the 1,963 pages saved while reviewing the rule, the sidebar rule clears 16 more short posts on community.joomla.org and two FC2 blogs. Of those and 321 more from the last review, the comment-form rule clears 22 short entries, from 12 more FC2 blogs, a Tistory blog and an N-DSoft news site, two of which are then too short to summarize, with no other verdict changing. No saved page needs the blog-name rule yet: it is there for a blog whose name links home beside a sign-in form that isn't a comment form.

## Translated summaries

99. **A summary can be translated in the reader into one of the interface languages other than its own, one language per summary for now.** Simplified and Traditional Chinese count as one language: a Chinese summary can go to English or French, and an English or French one to either Chinese. A summary in another language, such as German, can go to any of the four. The limit is `MAX_TRANSLATIONS_PER_SUMMARY` in `apps/web/src/lib/summarize/languages.ts`, and the table and the reader already handle more. A translation is asked for, never made automatically: a summary is usually read in its own language, and each translation is a Claude call.
100. **Translations are a table of their own (`summary_translations`), one row per summary and language,** made by a `translate_summary` job, as summaries are. They belong to a summary, not to an article, so each model's summary on Compare has its own, and showing another model's summary shows that one's translations. Lists and Triage keep showing the summary in its own language; the reader shows the translation under it, marked with its language. Search finds an article by its translations, and the export includes them.
101. **The model that wrote the summary translates it, at the summaries' effort** (`SUMMARY_EFFORT`, low by default), with the same refusal fallback, time limit and cost tracking: the owner chose that model for the article, and translating a few sentences is easy work. An answer with a different number of key points is tried again, since the points would no longer match the original's.
102. **A summary written again is translated again,** in the same transaction that stores it, and a translation into what is now the summary's own language goes. While a summary is being written again, its translation's job waits, and storing the new summary makes that job due at once. Each translation names the one job that may write it (`job_id`), so a job still translating the old text when the summary changes finishes without changing anything.
103. **Traditional Chinese translations ask for Taiwan's usage,** with examples of Taiwan's words against mainland and Hong Kong ones, Taiwan's punctuation and 「」, and say not to convert Simplified characters. Summaries of Chinese articles keep the article's script and regional usage and report it in their language tag (`zh-Hans` or `zh-Hant`), so the same-language rule can tell them apart.
104. **A failed job and its row record the failure in one transaction.** Before, the job could end while the row it updates (an article, a summary or a translation) stayed pending or running for good, if recording the row's side failed. Now both roll back, and the job is taken again once its lock goes stale.
105. **A translation names the model that wrote it under its heading, next to Remove, and not its cost.** The reader is for reading the summary; what each answer cost is recorded for reports instead (105).
106. **Every answer Claude gives is recorded in `ai_usage`:** who it was for, the article and summary, whether it was a summary or a translation and into which language, the model asked and the one that answered, the effort, tokens, estimated cost, time and stop reason. A summary's or a translation's own row keeps only its latest answer, so it can't say what was spent once it is written again or removed. Unusable answers (declined, cut off, in the wrong format) are recorded too, since they are billed, and so is what a stream had used when it broke off (a time-out, an error mid-answer); a request that got no answer at all (an API error before streaming) is not. `askClaude()` reports each answer before checking it, through `onAnswer`, which every caller must pass. The rows stay when their article is deleted, even while Claude is still answering, and go with their user. Summaries and translations saved before the table existed were copied into it from their own rows, with their latest usable answer only.
107. **Claude may translate one summary at most three times** (`MAX_TRANSLATION_RUNS_PER_SUMMARY`), in all languages together, counting retries, asking again after Remove and translating again after the summary is written again, failed answers included. A run counts from the moment its job takes it, before Claude is asked, with the summary locked so two jobs can't both take the last one; it is given back only if the request got no answer. So runs still with Claude count too. Once the limit is reached, the reader says so where it would offer a language or Retry, a failing translation stops trying again, a job stops before calling Claude, and a summary written again marks its translation failed rather than keeping one of the old text. Without it, Remove and Translate could be repeated without end, each time at the owner's cost.

## Admins

108. **Admins are the emails in `ADMIN_EMAILS`,** who must be in `ALLOWED_EMAILS` too. Only they see a summary's cost and time in the app, Compare models, writing a summary again with another model, comparison mode in Settings and the AI usage report. For everyone else those are gone: Compare and the report answer 404 (their page titles too), the actions behind them refuse, and comparison mode is ignored even if it was saved. A save or Retry of theirs runs only the summary they are shown, never again the other models of an earlier copy, so only an admin's saves pay for every model. Each user's JSON export still has the costs of their own summaries (41). A variable, like `ALLOWED_EMAILS`, is enough for an owner and a few readers; a role in the database would need a screen to manage it.
109. **The AI usage report (`/admin/usage`) adds up `ai_usage` for each user by week, month or year:** answers (summaries and translations), tokens, estimated cost, how many answers have no known price (a model the app has no price for, or a run still under way), and time, in minutes or hours once it adds up. Periods are in UTC and weeks start on Monday, whatever the database's own time zone, so every admin sees the same totals. It covers the last 12 weeks, the last 12 months, or every year, newest first and the costliest user first.

## Colors

110. **The brand's colors are ink and cinnabar, after the logo,** instead of the green the app started with, which many reading apps already use, as Apple Books uses orange. Wherever the logo stands among other apps' icons (home screens, the browser's toolbar and tabs), it is cinnabar (`#a3362a`) with pale strokes. Inside the app it is pale strokes on ink (`#2b2723`) with the bookmark in cinnabar (`#c8412f`): inscriptions have long been passed on as ink rubbings, pale characters on black, and some oracle bones had their carved strokes filled with cinnabar. The default palettes take ink as their accent, Paper and White in `#2b2723`, and bone in the dark, Graphite in `#e3d8c1` and Black in `#d9ceb7`. Cinnabar is kept out of the interface, since errors are red and a red Save button would read as a warning. With an accent as dark or as light as the text, hovering moves it toward the page rather than the text, so the change still shows (`paletteTokens()`). The web app's manifest starts the browser's bars in Paper's page color, which each page then sets for itself (81). The extension's badge after a save and its options page use ink and bone too. Indigo, the blue of old thread-bound books' covers, and plum were tried as well: blue is the most common color among apps and close to the Mist, Slate and Midnight palettes, and plum has no tie to books.

## The first deploy

111. **The web app keeps jsdom at 26.1.0,** the last release whose dependencies all load with `require()`. Vercel's Node runtime starts with `require()` of ES modules turned off (`--no-experimental-require-module`), which a local Node 22.12 or later allows, and Next.js leaves jsdom in `node_modules` for the server to load at run time. From jsdom 27 on, its `html-encoding-sniffer` requires the ES-only `@exodus/bytes`, so on the first deploy every page of the app and the save and job routes failed, while the tests and local builds passed. `src/test/vercel-runtime.test.ts` loads jsdom and pg with Vercel's flags, so an upgrade that brings this back fails the tests. The extension's tests keep jsdom 30, since they never run on Vercel.

## Pages hidden until their scripts run

112. **When Readability finds no article, it reads the page again with what an inline `visibility: hidden` hides shown,** since the app never runs a page's scripts. Wenxuecity hides both the desktop and the mobile copy of each news story that way until its script shows the one that fits the screen, so the app found no article in any of them. Pages where Readability finds an article are read as before, so hidden newsletters, menus and pop-ups stay out of them, and elements hidden with `display: none` stay hidden, since that is how pop-ups and closed menus usually hide. Readability changes the page it reads, so the copy for the second try is made only when the page has such an element. Of 20 Wenxuecity news stories, all 20 are now read whole, in one copy, and none of the 1,963 pages saved for the login-page rules changes.

## Saved addresses

113. **Every address that led to an article is remembered (`article_urls`), and a save matches any of them.** An article's duplicate key can change after the download, to the page's canonical address or to where redirects ended, and a save looked up only the current key, so saving the same shared link or short link again made a second copy. Now the link as saved, the canonical address and the address the download landed on all lead to the article. An address another article already has stays with that one, so two articles are never merged, and a copy the job throws away (114) adds no address. The cost is a link that leads somewhere new each time, such as a "latest issue" address: saving it again finds the first article instead of saving the new page. Articles saved before the table existed got their own key and their link without its fragment, the nearest SQL gets to the app's normalizing, with the oldest article keeping a link two of them share; a link that differed from its key only by tracking parameters still isn't matched. Addresses over 2,000 bytes aren't remembered, since Postgres can't index them. The migration has to run before the code that uses the table is deployed (docs/setup/neon.md).
114. **Saving a "Preview only", failed or empty copy again by its address downloads it again,** and the job keeps whichever copy is better: a complete one over any preview, a preview that gets summarized (a teaser, a subscriber-only page's free part) over one that doesn't (a login page, no article, too short), and within the same kind the longer one, the stored copy when they tie. So saving a preview again asks Claude for new summaries, and uses a translation run (107), only when the page now gives more. No download is queued while one is running, since it reads the page as it is now, and one waiting to try again is made due at once, as Retry does; the reply says the copy is being downloaded again. A download that fails leaves a copy that has content as it was and readable, with the failure kept on the job only. A complete copy is only moved to the top.

## A description that holds the article

115. **When fewer than 100 words of a subscriber-only article are free, its description takes their place, as far as the marked sections hold it from its start, if that is 100 words or more.** It is kept in paragraphs where the description leaves a blank line, with its other line breaks kept. The publisher shows the description to anyone, in search results and link previews, outside the sections it marks, so the sections are still dropped as the brief requires: their text, without scripts and whitespace, is only searched for the description. That keeps a long blurb about the site from being taken for the article, and leaves out what a publisher adds after the text, such as LinkedIn's count of comments. LinkedIn marks a post and its comments as not free, and covers them with a sign-in prompt after a few seconds for a visitor who isn't signed in, yet its description holds the whole post: a server download now keeps the post and summarizes it, still flagged "Preview only", where it kept nothing before. The copy the extension saves from a signed-in browser is still the full one, with its links and formatting. Of the 84 saved pages with paywall markup, only the three LinkedIn posts change: on the others part of the article is free, or the description has 49 words at most.

## Bot checks

116. **A server download is taken for a bot check when its page has fewer than 100 words and 20 links, no password field, paywall markup or paywall by name, and its scripts set a cookie and then reload the page or go to another address, or reload right after reading a cookie. Nothing of it is kept, and it is flagged "Preview only".** Juejin answers every download that hasn't passed ByteDance's check with such a page: a script works out a number, sets it as a cookie and reloads, and the page says only "Please wait...". The app stored that as the article. Google's reCAPTCHA check, which NCBI's Bookshelf and PubMed Central send, sets its cookie itself, and the page's own script tests for it and reloads in the same `if`. Reading a cookie counts only with a reload that close, within 200 characters: a script that reads one and goes to another address is more often an app's routing, as on Feishu's documentation, and every Wix site reads its cookies and, thousands of characters on, reloads a page the browser brings back from its history. Working the answer out on the server would be getting around the check, which the brief rules out, and a browser passes it by itself, so the notice sends the owner to the extension. Nothing the check page names is kept either, neither its title ("Just a moment..."), language, description, author or date, nor its canonical address or where its redirects ended: the title is the one sent with the save, or the site's address. Only scripts a browser would run from the page count, wherever on the page the cookie and the move are, and a bare `location =` only with a window before it, since it is as often a variable of that name or part of a query. The page has to be nearly blank because most other pages' scripts set cookies and move on too: with a limit on the article's words alone, 19 of the 24 saved pages the rule caught were real, among them CNN's and Crikey's subscriber-only articles and short home pages, and a limit on the page's words alone still caught short etoland forum posts. Of 2,815 pages saved for these rules or downloaded for this one, 16 change, all checks or interstitials: Juejin, Ixigua, Tmall, BOSS直聘, PubMed, Yandex, banki.ru, eyny, NGA, derStandard's cookie consent and a "Just a moment" page; counting a reload right after a cookie is read adds 2 of 2,818, the reCAPTCHA pages of NCBI and PubMed Central, and catches 20 more NCBI, PubMed and PubChem pages downloaded for it, while none of 87 Wix pages changes. A copy saved before keeps "Please wait..." until the page is saved from the browser, since both copies hold nothing worth summarizing and the stored one is longer (114). Checks the rule doesn't catch are stored as before: those served with a 401, 402 or 403 keep the refusal's reason and their page, and others, which set the cookie on the server or in obfuscated code, or submit a form (Reddit, Weibo, Anubis, AWS WAF, 36kr), still read as a short page or no article.

## The name and address

117. **The app is called ReadSooner, written as one word, at readsooner.com.** Its words are ones every reader knows, including those who use the app in French or Chinese, and it plays on "sooner rather than later" against the read-later apps it follows: the summary lets you take in an article sooner instead of leaving it in a pile. Its .com was free, so the name and the address people guess are the same. GistLater, a candidate in the brief, puts the summaries first, but many readers don't know the word "gist", "gist later" reads backwards (the gist comes now, the article later), and GitHub Gist fills searches for it. Laternook was free everywhere, but NOOK is Barnes & Noble's e-reader. ReadSoon is as plain, but a broker holds its .com; LaterRead turns the usual phrase around, and its .com was waiting to be deleted rather than free. ReadKit and SaveToRead are already read-later apps. The repository, the `@read-later` packages, the `readlater` database and its backups, and the add-on ID (76) keep the working name, Read Later: a reader never sees them, and renaming the database or the add-on ID would break what is already set up.
118. **The app's address is `https://www.readsooner.com`, and `readsooner.com` redirects to it** with a 308 that keeps the path. Vercel recommends `www` as the main domain, since its CNAME lets Vercel choose where traffic goes, while a bare domain is tied to fixed addresses, which Vercel reports timing out from some networks. People can still type and say `readsooner.com`. The domain is registered at Namecheap, with its DNS on Cloudflare like the owner's other domains, and because the Cloudflare Tunnel that the move to one server uses needs the DNS there. Both names are CNAMEs to the project's own Vercel target, DNS only; Cloudflare answers for the bare one with the target's addresses. `APP_URL`, Google's redirect URIs, the cron Worker, the extension and the Shortcuts all use the `www` address, because a browser drops the `Authorization` header when a request is redirected to another host, so a token sent to the bare domain would be lost.

## The reader's actions and the way back

119. **The reading column stays 40rem (640 px) wide.** At the article's 19 px it holds about 65 to 70 characters a line in the classic serif, and about 60 in the wider Merriweather and Atkinson Hyperlegible; Chinese at 17.6 px fits about 36 characters. That is inside the 45 to 75 characters that Bringhurst gives for a single column, near his ideal of 66, so every reading font reads comfortably without a separate width setting. On phones, every script fits fewer characters a line than on a larger screen (about 40 Latin or 20 Chinese characters), which is normal on a screen held that close.
120. **The reader has one action bar, shown twice: under the title and at the end of the article,** in the same order both times: Archive (or Move to Inbox), Favorite, Mark unread, Folder, and Delete at the far end. Before, Archive, Favorite and Mark unread were at the top, and Folder and Delete at the bottom. Phones show only the icons, which the labels still name for screen readers. Favorite is one toggle button with `aria-pressed`, rather than a label that changes. "Open original" isn't something done to the saved copy, so it moved into the line of details under the title and next to the saved date at the end. Folder opens a menu in the browser's popover, as the "Aa" menu does: a panel in the middle of the screen, or a sheet at the bottom on phones, where choosing a folder moves the article at once. Browsers without popovers show the menu as a plain section at the end of the article.
121. **Back goes to the list the article was opened from, as it was left.** Lists link to an article with their own address in `from` (`/articles/<id>?from=/favorites?tag=css`), search, folder, tag and page included, and the reader accepts only the four lists there (`apps/web/src/lib/reader-origin.ts`). An article opened any other way, from the extension, a bookmark or a link, goes back to the list it is in: the Archive if it is archived, else the Inbox. While reading, that list's tab stays marked and leads back to it. When the list opened the reader in the same tab and nothing was opened from the reader since, Back is the browser's Back, which shows the list scrolled where it was, and the list then refreshes itself to show what changed; otherwise Back opens the list. There is no separate back link at the top, which would repeat the marked tab right above it.
122. **A round Back button floats beside the article** for when the header has scrolled away. The installed app has no browser Back button, and a long article ended far from the tabs. On screens wide enough to leave a margin (840 px and up) it sits beside the column near the top of the window and never covers the text, with CSS alone. On narrower screens it floats in the bottom-left corner, within reach of a thumb, and it would cover text there, so a small script hides it while the tabs are on screen and while the page scrolls down, and shows it when the page scrolls up or reaches the end. A second click within 600 ms of Back is ignored, both on the reader and on the list it lands on: the second click of a double-click would otherwise go back twice and leave the app, or press whatever on the list is under the button, such as a card's Archive on a phone.
123. **Archive, Mark unread and Delete in the reader go back to the list; Favorite, Folder and Move to Inbox stay.** The first three are done with the article, as "mark unread" is in email. They don't refresh the reader they are leaving (a refresh after Delete showed "not found" for a moment), and the list catches up when it is shown again. Mark unread in the reader used to be undone at once: the refresh after it told the reader the article was unread, and the reader marked it read again. The reader now marks an article read only once each time it is opened (34).

## Opening sign-in

124. **Sign-in is opened to everyone by setting `ALLOWED_EMAILS` to `*`, and `BLOCKED_EMAILS` keeps particular accounts out.** An empty or missing `ALLOWED_EMAILS` still lets no one in, so a mistake in the settings closes the app rather than opening it. A blocked account is locked out as one taken off the list is (15), from pages, sign-ins and access tokens, and its queued downloads, summaries and translations stop without running, so blocking an account also stops what it costs. The owner opened sign-in before stage 2's monthly summary limit and stage 3's privacy page, terms and account deletion, watching the AI usage report (109) instead and adding the limit when it is needed. Admins still come only from `ADMIN_EMAILS` (108).

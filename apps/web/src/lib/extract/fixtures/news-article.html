The quiet return of community orchards

By Dana Whitfield

On the edge of Millbrook, between a bus depot and a row of allotments, forty young apple trees stand in four neat lines. Five years ago the strip of land was rough grass that the council mowed twice a summer. Today it is one of more than sixty community orchards that have been planted on public land across the region, and the people who look after it say the trees have changed the street as much as the soil.

The idea is not new. Parish orchards were common until the middle of the last century, when many were cleared for housing or simply left to decline. What is new is the way the modern versions are run. Most are planted by volunteers on land that stays in public hands, and the fruit belongs to whoever turns up to pick it. A few have formal rotas; most rely on a noticeboard and a group chat.

"People assume the hard part is the planting," said Priya Natarajan, who coordinates volunteers at Millbrook. "The planting takes a weekend. The hard part is the next ten years: the watering in a dry spring, the pruning in winter, and teaching people that you leave the tree in better shape than you found it." She keeps a binder of pruning diagrams in the boot of her car and hands out photocopies to anyone who asks.

Why councils are saying yes

For local authorities the attraction is partly financial. An orchard, once established, needs less mowing than a lawn, and volunteers do much of the remaining work. Several councils have also found that orchards reduce complaints about anti-social behaviour, because a space that people visit regularly is a space that people keep an eye on. A report published last spring by a regional parks partnership found that sites with fruit trees received roughly a third fewer litter reports than comparable open grassland.

There are environmental benefits too. Older varieties of apple, pear and plum support a wider range of insects than ornamental planting, and the long grass that is often left beneath the trees gives shelter to small mammals. Some groups have started to graft cuttings from the last surviving trees of local varieties, so that names like Millbrook Russet and Harper's Late are not lost.

Not every project succeeds. Trees planted without a plan for watering often die in their second summer, and orchards that depend on a single enthusiastic organiser can fade when that person moves away. Groups that last tend to share the work widely and to keep the fruit genuinely free. "The moment it feels like somebody's private garden, people stop coming," Natarajan said.

There is also the question of what to do with a glut. A mature apple tree can produce more fruit than a street can eat, and windfalls left on the ground attract wasps. Millbrook now runs a pressing day each October, borrowing a hand press from a neighbouring village. Last year the volunteers made nearly three hundred litres of juice, which they gave away in bottles collected from local cafés.

What comes next

The regional partnership hopes to see a hundred orchards planted by the end of the decade, and it has published a short guide for groups that want to start one. The advice is practical: choose varieties suited to the local soil, plant in the dormant season, protect young trunks from deer and mowers, and agree in advance who will water during the first two summers. Back at Millbrook, the trees are now tall enough to cast shade. On a recent Saturday a retired engineer was showing two teenagers how to check a branch for canker, while a toddler tried to carry an apple that was nearly the size of her head. Natarajan watched them from the gate. "In thirty years these will be big old trees," she said. "Most of the people who planted them won't be here to see it. That's sort of the point."

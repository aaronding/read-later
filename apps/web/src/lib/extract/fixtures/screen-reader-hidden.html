The last repair shop for old radios

Behind a green door on Mill Lane, Ruth Okafor has spent thirty years taking apart radios that their owners were told could not be saved. Her shelves hold valves in paper sleeves, coils wound by hand and a drawer of knobs sorted by colour, and the waiting list for a repair now runs to four months.

Most of the sets that arrive were made between the 1930s and the 1960s. Some come from collectors, but more come from families who found a grandparent's radio in a loft and want to hear it play again, even if only once, before they decide what to do with it.

Okafor learned the trade from her father, who opened the shop after the war and repaired televisions as well until the work became too fiddly for his eyes. She kept the radios and let the televisions go, and she still uses his test bench, with its brass terminals worn smooth by decades of use.

The hardest part of the work is finding parts. The factories that made the valves closed long ago, and the stock that remains is spread across sheds and garages around the country. Okafor buys whole radios at auctions when they hold a valve she needs, and she trades with a dozen other repairers by post, swapping what each of them has too much of.

Parts nobody makes any more

Capacitors are a different problem. The old paper ones dry out and leak, and a radio switched on after decades in a loft can burn out its transformer in minutes. Okafor replaces them with modern parts hidden inside the old cases, so that a set looks as it did when it left the factory but will not catch fire on a shelf.

Then there is the sound. Owners often expect a repaired radio to sound like a modern speaker, and Okafor has learned to warn them that it will not. A set from 1938 was built for a small room and a single station, she tells them, and it will sound warm and narrow and a little distant, which is exactly what most of them were hoping to hear once they hear it.

She has trained two apprentices in the past five years, and one of them now runs a shop of his own across the river. The other still works with her three days a week. Okafor says the shop will stay open as long as people keep bringing in radios, and so far they have never stopped coming, whatever the weather or the state of the world outside.
